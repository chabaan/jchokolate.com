<p>The smell of butter hitting a hot skillet. The soft hiss as the bread makes contact. A whiff of cinnamon and chocolate mixing with campfire smoke. That’s the moment this breakfast becomes something more than just fuel for the trail.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/250c3465-f7ac-40b6-a5b0-883ab11af380/0_1_640_N.webp" alt="Bread slices soaking in a shallow dish of egg mixture with chocolate chips scattered on top" /></figure>
<p>Chocolate chip French toast sounds like a luxury, but it’s actually one of the simplest camp breakfasts to get right — if you know the one trick that keeps it from turning into a burnt, soggy mess. I’ve made it over a single-burner camp stove, on a grate above coals, and even on a portable grill. The method stays the same. Here’s the secret.</p><h2>The Bread Matters More Than You Think</h2><p>Thin, flimsy sandwich bread is the enemy of good French toast. It soaks up the custard too fast and falls apart before the inside cooks. At camp, you want bread that can take a little rough handling.</p><p>Look for thick-cut slices — at least one inch. Brioche, challah, Texas toast, or a day-old sourdough all work beautifully. Stale bread is actually better: it absorbs the custard evenly without turning to mush. If your bread is fresh, leave it unwrapped on the counter overnight. Or, if you forgot, toast it lightly over the fire before dipping.</p><h2>The Custard: Simple Ratios, Big Impact</h2><p>The custard is where most people go wrong. Too much milk and you get a soggy interior. Too few eggs and it won’t set properly. I use a straightforward ratio: one large egg per two slices of bread, with about a quarter cup of milk per egg. That gives you a custard that clings to the bread without pooling in the pan.</p><p>Whisk the eggs and milk together until smooth. Add a tablespoon of brown sugar per egg — it adds a deeper sweetness than white sugar. A splash of vanilla and a good pinch of cinnamon round out the flavor. Don’t skip the salt; a tiny amount balances the sweetness and keeps the chocolate from tasting flat.</p><h2>The One Trick That Changes Everything</h2><p>Here’s the secret: <strong>soak the bread, then add the chocolate chips to one side only — and cook chocolate-side up first.</strong></p><p>Most recipes tell you to mix the chips into the custard or sprinkle them on after cooking. Both methods have problems. Mixing them in means the chips sink to the bottom of the dish, leaving you with uneven distribution. Adding them after cooking means they never melt into the bread.</p><p>Instead, dip each slice quickly — about 10 to 15 seconds per side. Place the soaked slice on a plate and scatter a generous pinch of mini chocolate chips (or finely chopped dark chocolate) onto one side. Press them gently so they stick. Then lay the slice in the hot, buttered skillet with the chocolate side facing up. The exposed top allows the chips to soften and melt slowly as the bottom browns. When you flip, the chocolate side hits the pan for just a minute — long enough to form a thin, caramelized crust without burning.</p><p>Mini chips work best because they melt faster and distribute more evenly. If you only have regular chips, chop them into smaller pieces. Dark chocolate (60-70% cacao) adds a nice contrast to the sweet custard.</p><h2>Heat Management on a Camp Stove or Fire</h2><p>Cast iron holds heat like a battery. That’s great for even cooking, but it also means you need to watch your temperature. Medium heat on a camp stove is usually enough. If you’re cooking over coals, let the fire burn down to a steady bed of hot embers — no flames licking the pan.</p><p>Test the pan: drop a tiny piece of butter in. It should sizzle gently, not brown instantly. If it burns, your pan is too hot. Lift it off the heat for a minute and let it cool slightly.</p><p>Cook each slice for about three to four minutes on the first side. The bottom should be deep golden brown, not pale. Flip carefully — the chocolate side will be soft. Cook for another two to three minutes, then transfer to a plate. If you’re making a batch, keep finished slices warm by wrapping them in foil near the fire or in a low oven if you have one.</p><h2>Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them</h2><ul><li><strong>Over-soaking the bread.</strong> This is the biggest cause of soggy French toast. A quick dip is all you need. If the bread feels heavy and wet, you’ve gone too far.</li><li><strong>Using cold butter.</strong> Cold butter doesn’t coat the pan evenly. Let it soften at room temperature or cut it into small pieces so it melts quickly.</li><li><strong>Not letting the pan come to temperature.</strong> A cold pan soaks up butter and leaves the bread greasy. Wait until the butter sizzles before adding the bread.</li><li><strong>Forgetting to clean the pan between batches.</strong> Burnt butter and chocolate residue will ruin the next round. Wipe the skillet clean with a paper towel (careful — it’s hot) and add fresh butter.</li></ul><h2>Adapting for Wind, Cold, and Crowds</h2><p>A gusty day can make temperature control a nightmare. Set up a windbreak — a rock ring, a cooler turned on its side, or even your backpack. If the stove flame keeps blowing out, use a windscreen (but leave ventilation for the burner).</p><p>Cold weather means the custard and butter will stiffen quickly. Keep the milk and eggs in a thermos or insulated bottle until you’re ready to mix. If the butter won’t spread, just drop small pats directly into the pan as it heats.</p><p>Cooking for a group? Use a large griddle or two skillets. Soak all the bread at once, layering it with parchment paper to prevent sticking. Cook in batches and keep the finished slices warm in a foil packet.</p><h2>Takeaway</h2><p>Chocolate chip French toast at camp doesn’t have to be a gamble. Thick bread, a quick soak, and the chocolate-side-up trick give you a breakfast that’s crispy outside, tender inside, and full of melted pockets of chocolate. No burnt sugar, no raw center. Just a solid, satisfying start to a day outdoors.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Why does my French toast come out soggy in the middle?</h3>
<p>Soggy French toast usually means the bread is too thin or soaked too long. Use thick-cut bread (at least 1 inch) and dip just long enough to coat both sides — no more than 15 seconds per side. Also, make sure your pan is hot enough; a drop of water should sizzle on contact.</p>
<h3>Can I make chocolate chip French toast without a stove?</h3>
<p>Yes. You can cook it over a campfire grate or on a portable grill. Just place a cast iron skillet or griddle directly over the coals or grate. Keep the heat moderate — too hot and the chocolate burns before the egg sets.</p>
<h3>What’s the best bread for French toast at camp?</h3>
<p>Sturdy breads like brioche, challah, Texas toast, or day-old sourdough work best. They hold up to soaking and won't fall apart. Avoid soft sandwich bread — it turns to mush.</p>
