<p>The smell of chocolate and butter drifting through camp in the early morning is something I never get tired of. It draws people out of their tents like a magnet — sleepy-eyed, coffee mugs in hand, asking what's for breakfast. These chocolate chip baked donuts are the answer. No deep frying, no complicated equipment. Just one bowl, a donut pan, and a camp stove or Dutch oven.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/0aab1b38-ee9e-4512-88a1-d040c16ff957/0_1_640_N.webp" alt="Pouring donut batter into a greased donut pan using a piping bag" /></figure>
<h2>Why Baked Donuts Work at Camp</h2><p>Frying donuts at a campsite is a hassle. You need a deep pot, a thermometer, a mess of oil to dispose of, and constant attention to keep the temperature steady. Baked donuts skip all of that. They come together in about 20 minutes from start to finish, and they're lighter — tender rather than greasy. The texture is closer to a soft cake than a yeast donut, which means they hold up well in a backpack or on a plate next to a campfire.</p><h2>The One Trick That Makes These Donuts Better</h2><p>Most baked donut recipes tell you to fold the chocolate chips into the batter and call it done. Here's the thing — if you do that, the chips sink to the bottom of the donut while baking. You end up with a ring of chocolate stuck to the pan and a bare top. The fix is simple: reserve about a tablespoon of mini chocolate chips and sprinkle them on top of the batter right after you pipe it into the pan. That way, every donut has visible, melty chocolate on its crown. Mini chips work best here because they distribute evenly and don't weigh down the batter.</p><h2>Adapting for Camp Conditions</h2><h3>Baking on a Camp Stove</h3><p>If you don't have an oven at your campsite, use a Dutch oven. Place a small trivet or three clean, flat pebbles in the bottom of the Dutch oven to lift the donut pan off the direct heat. Preheat the Dutch oven with the lid on over low heat for 5 minutes. Arrange 6–8 hot charcoal briquettes on the lid and 4–5 underneath. Bake for 18–22 minutes, rotating the Dutch oven a quarter turn every 10 minutes for even heat. Check for doneness with a toothpick — it should come out clean or with a few moist crumbs.</p><h3>High Altitude Adjustments</h3><p>Camping above 5,000 feet? Reduce the baking powder to 3/4 teaspoon and increase the milk by 1 tablespoon. The lower air pressure makes baked goods rise too quickly, so the slight reduction keeps the donuts from doming and cracking on top.</p><h3>Cold Weather Baking</h3><p>If the temperature is below 40°F, your ingredients will be cold. Let the egg and milk sit out for 15 minutes before mixing. Cold batter doesn't rise as well, and the donuts can turn out dense. Also, preheat your Dutch oven or camp oven for an extra 5 minutes to compensate for heat loss.</p><h2>The Gear You Actually Need</h2><p>A 6-cavity non-stick donut pan is lightweight and nests flat in a tote. If you don't want to carry a dedicated pan, use a standard 12-cup muffin tin — fill each cup halfway and bake for 12–14 minutes. The shape is different, but the taste is identical. A silicone spatula and a piping bag (or a zip-top bag with a snipped corner) make filling the cavities mess-free. I've also used a small spoon, but the bag is faster and gives cleaner results.</p><h2>Making Ahead and Storing</h2><p>These donuts are best the day they're made, but they keep well for two days in an airtight container. If you're backpacking, you can mix the dry ingredients at home and carry them in a zip-top bag. Add the wet ingredients at camp. For car camping, I sometimes bake a batch at home and bring them along — they reheat nicely wrapped in foil and set near the coals for 5 minutes.</p><h2>A Note on Safety</h2><p>When using a camp stove or Dutch oven with charcoal, set it up on a stable, level surface away from tent walls, dry grass, and overhanging branches. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of water nearby. Never leave a hot stove unattended. And if you're baking inside a tent vestibule or a screened shelter, make sure there's cross-ventilation — carbon monoxide from any fuel-burning device can build up quickly.</p><p>These donuts are simple enough to make on a relaxed morning but special enough to feel like a treat. The chocolate chips melt into little pockets of sweetness, and the cinnamon adds a warmth that pairs perfectly with coffee. One batch makes six donuts — enough for two people to have two each, with two left over for the person who wakes up last.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I make these donuts without a donut pan?</h3>
<p>Yes. If you don't have a donut pan, use a standard muffin tin. Fill each cup about halfway. The texture will be more like a muffin top, but the flavor is the same. Reduce the bake time by 2–3 minutes and check for doneness with a toothpick.</p>
<h3>How do I bake donuts on a camp stove without an oven?</h3>
<p>Use a Dutch oven with a lid. Place the donut pan inside on a small trivet or a few clean pebbles to lift it off the bottom. Set the Dutch oven over low heat on the camp stove. Place 6–8 briquettes on the lid and 4–5 underneath. Rotate the Dutch oven a quarter turn every 10 minutes for even heat. Bake for about 18–22 minutes.</p>
<h3>Can I use a different type of chocolate chip?</h3>
<p>Absolutely. Mini chocolate chips distribute more evenly and don't sink as much. Dark, milk, or white chocolate chips all work. If you're in hot weather, use chocolate chips with a higher cocoa butter content (like 60% or more) so they hold their shape better during mixing.</p>
